# Mountain Biking in Adventure Racing

<br/>

## Introduction

Mountain biking is a core discipline in most adventure races, offering a welcome change of pace from trekking while covering distance more efficiently. However, biking in adventure racing is quite different from recreational mountain biking or road cycling. You'll face navigation challenges, varied terrain, equipment issues, and the need to stay with your team—all while managing energy for the other race disciplines and hours of racing ahead.

This guide covers everything beginners need to know about the mountain biking portion of adventure racing, from choosing and setting up your bike to pacing strategies, team dynamics, and handling common mechanical issues.

<br/>

## How Biking Works in Adventure Races

### What to Expect
- **Varied terrain**: Forest roads, singletrack, doubletrack, gravel paths, technical sections, and sometimes pavement
- **Navigation while riding**: You'll need to check maps and make route decisions on the bike
- **Team pacing**: Riding together as a team, not at individual max speeds
- **Checkpoint hunting**: Bikes may need to be stashed while teams hike short distances to reach checkpoints off the trail
- **Self-sufficiency**: All repairs and mechanical issues must be handled by your team

### Differences from Recreational Mountain Biking
- **Duration**: You may bike for 2-8+ hours at a time, requiring sustainable pacing
- **Equipment failures**: Mechanical issues are common and must be fixed on the trail
- **Team coordination**: Stronger riders must pace with slower teammates
- **Navigation**: You're constantly checking maps and making route decisions
- **Multi-sport fatigue**: You're biking after trekking or before paddling, not with fresh legs

## Choosing the Right Bike

### Bike Types

**Cross-Country (XC) Mountain Bike** ✅ Recommended for beginners
- Lightweight and efficient
- Front suspension (100-120mm travel)
- Designed for all-day riding
- Great balance of speed and comfort
- Best for: Most adventure races

**Trail Mountain Bike**
- Slightly more suspension (120-150mm travel)
- More comfortable on rough terrain
- A bit heavier but still efficient
- Good option for technical courses

**Full-Suspension vs. Hardtail**
- **Hardtail (front suspension only)**: Lighter, simpler to maintain, more efficient on climbs, less expensive
- **Full-suspension (front + rear)**: More comfortable on rough terrain, better control on descents, slightly heavier

**For Beginners**: A hardtail XC or trail bike is an excellent starting point. It's lighter, mechanically simpler, and adequate for most races.

### Bike Fit and Setup

**Proper Fit is Critical**:
- **Saddle height**: When pedaling, your leg should be almost straight (slight bend) at the bottom of the stroke
- **Handlebar position**: Comfortable reach without straining back or shoulders
- **Test with full gear**: Adjust with your race pack and hydration system on, as they affect posture

**Frame Size**:
- Should allow you to stand over the top tube with 2-3 inches of clearance
- If between sizes, smaller is often better for maneuverability

### Suspension Setup
- **Air pressure/preload**: Adjust for your weight and terrain
- **Softer (lower pressure)**: Better for technical, rooty, or rocky trails
- **Firmer (higher pressure)**: Better for smooth, fast-rolling trails and climbs
- **Test before the race**: Dial in your suspension during training rides

## Tire Choice and Setup

### Tire Width
- **2.1"–2.4" width**: Ideal balance for adventure racing
- Narrower (2.0"-2.1"): Faster rolling, less grip
- Wider (2.3"-2.4"): More comfort and traction, slightly slower
- Avoid extremely wide (2.5"+) or downhill-specific tires unless conditions demand them

### Tread Patterns

**For Hardpack/Dry Conditions**:
- Closely-spaced, low-profile knobs
- Faster rolling, less drag

**For Loose/Muddy Conditions**:
- Taller, widely-spaced knobs
- Better grip in mud and loose terrain
- Slightly slower on hardpack

**All-Around Choice**: A medium tread pattern works well for most adventure races with mixed terrain.

### Tire Pressure
Starting points (adjust for rider weight and terrain):
- **Front tire**: 20–26 psi
- **Rear tire**: 22–30 psi (slightly higher than front)

**Lighter riders**: Can go lower for more grip
**Heavier riders**: Need higher pressure to prevent pinch flats

**Too high**: Harsh ride, less grip, skittish handling
**Too low**: Risk of pinch flats, burping (tubeless), sluggish feeling

### Tubeless Setup ✅ Highly Recommended
**Benefits**:
- Self-seals small punctures
- Allows lower tire pressure for better grip
- Reduces pinch flats
- Lighter than tubes

**Requirements**:
- Tubeless-ready rims and tires
- Tubeless sealant (replenish every 2-3 months)
- Tubeless valve stems
- Carry spare tubes and a tubeless plug kit as backup

## Essential Repair Kit

### Bare Minimum (Mandatory)
- **1-2 spare tubes** (even if running tubeless)
- **Tire levers** (2-3 plastic levers)
- **Pump or CO₂ inflator** (CO₂ is faster but carry backup)
- **Multi-tool** with:
  - Allen keys (hex wrenches) for your bike's sizes
  - Chain breaker tool
  - Screwdrivers
- **Quick links** (2-3 spare chain links)
- **Tubeless plug kit** (if running tubeless)
- **Patch kit** for tubes

### Highly Recommended
- **Spare derailleur hanger** (specific to your bike model—very fragile part)
- **Duct tape** (wrap around seat post or carry small roll)
- **Zip ties** (5-10, various sizes)
- **Small bottle of chain lube**

### Organizing Your Kit
- Store in a small bag under your saddle or in your frame bag
- Keep frequently-needed items accessible
- Test your repair kit on training rides—make sure you can actually use everything

## Common Mechanical Issues and Fixes

### Flat Tires (Most Common)

**With Tubeless**:
1. Inspect tire for object stuck in tread
2. Remove sharp object
3. Use tubeless plug to seal larger holes
4. Re-inflate with pump or CO₂
5. If plug doesn't work, install a tube

**With Tubes**:
1. Remove wheel
2. Use tire levers to remove tire from rim
3. Replace or patch tube
4. Check inside tire for thorns, glass, or sharp objects
5. Reinstall tire and inflate

### Dropped Chain
- **Prevention**: Shift to easier gears before stopping; maintain proper chain tension
- **Fix**: Stop, lift chain back onto chainring, wipe hands on pants, continue riding
- **Tip**: Practice this during training—it's quick once you know how

### Derailleur Issues
- **Shifting poorly**: Barrel adjuster tweaks (on shifter or derailleur) can fix minor issues
- **Chain won't shift to certain gears**: Cable tension may need adjustment
- **Bent derailleur hanger**: Can sometimes be carefully bent back or need replacement
- **Practice**: Ask a bike shop to show you basic derailleur adjustments

### Broken Chain
- **Fix**: Use chain breaker on multi-tool to remove broken link, reconnect with quick link
- **Result**: Rideable but may need to avoid certain gear combinations
- **Practice**: Break and fix a chain at home before race day

### Brake Problems
- **Squealing**: Usually cosmetic, doesn't affect performance
- **Weak braking**: Check brake pads for wear; adjust cable tension (mechanical) or bleed (hydraulic—rarely needed mid-race)
- **Rubbing**: Wheel may be slightly untrue; can sometimes be fixed by adjusting brake calipers

## Pacing Strategy

### Think Long-Term
Adventure races are endurance events, not sprints. Your biking pace should be sustainable for hours.

**Guidelines**:
- **Conversational pace**: You should be able to talk to teammates while riding
- **Save energy for later**: It's better to finish strong than burn out early
- **Adjust for terrain**: Ride efficiently on flats and downhills; don't be afraid to walk steep or technical climbs

### Managing Effort
- **Avoid the "bike high"**: It's easy to go too hard on the bike after a tough trekking section—resist this temptation
- **Monitor heart rate**: If you have a heart rate monitor, stay in zone 2-3 for most of the ride
- **Walk when strategic**: Pushing the bike up a steep climb may be faster and save energy

### Nutrition and Hydration
- **Eat regularly**: Every 20-30 minutes, even if you don't feel hungry
- **Drink frequently**: Easier to drink on the bike than while trekking
- **Don't wait for hunger or thirst**: By then, you're already behind

For more details, see our [Nutrition Guide](/content/nutrition.md).

## Team Riding Dynamics

### Staying Together
Most races require teams to stay within sight (often 100 meters). On bikes, this can be challenging when speeds vary.

**Strategies**:
- **Regroup frequently**: Stronger riders wait at trail intersections, hills, or obvious landmarks
- **Call out stops**: Yell "stopping" so teammates behind you know
- **Ride in a line**: Single-file on singletrack; allows easier regrouping

### Drafting and Paceline Riding
On wider trails or roads, riding in a paceline saves energy:
- **Take turns at the front**: Rotate every 2-5 minutes
- **Communicate**: Call out "pulling off" when you're ready to rotate back
- **Stay smooth**: Avoid sudden accelerations or braking
- **Benefit**: Drafting can save 20-30% effort for riders behind the leader

### Supporting Slower Riders
- **Carry extra gear**: Stronger riders can take weight from struggling teammates
- **Provide encouragement**: Stay positive and motivating
- **Push uphill**: A gentle hand on the back can help a tired teammate on climbs
- **Check in frequently**: "How are you doing?" "Need anything?"

### Navigation While Riding
- **Assign a primary navigator**: One person checks the map while riding
- **Stop for complex decisions**: Pull over to discuss major route choices
- **Call out turns**: "Left in 50 meters" gives teammates time to prepare
- **Use handrails**: Follow obvious features (roads, trails, power lines) when possible

For more on navigation, see our [Navigation Guide](/content/navigation.md).

## Training for Adventure Racing Mountain Biking

### Build Endurance
- **Long rides**: 2-4+ hours at conversational pace
- **Back-to-back days**: Ride on consecutive days to simulate multi-leg racing
- **Mixed terrain**: Practice on gravel, singletrack, technical sections, and climbs

### Develop Skills
- **Cornering**: Lean the bike, not your body; look through the turn
- **Climbing**: Shift to easier gears before the hill; stay seated for traction
- **Descending**: Weight back, elbows out, look ahead (not at obstacles)
- **Technical features**: Practice riding over roots, rocks, and drops

### Practice Navigation
- Combine rides with map-reading practice
- Ride in unfamiliar areas and navigate to specific points
- Practice checking maps while riding (safely)

### Mechanical Skills
- Learn to fix a flat tire
- Practice using your multi-tool
- Break and repair a chain at home
- Adjust your derailleur

### Ride with Your Team
- Train together to learn each other's pace and strengths
- Practice communication and teamwork
- Test your race setup and gear

<br/>

## Race Day Bike Tips

### Pre-Race
- **Bike check**: Inspect tires, brakes, chain, bolts, and suspension
- **Test shifting**: Make sure all gears work smoothly
- **Pre-ride if allowed**: Familiarize yourself with the bike course section near the start
- **Check mandatory gear**: Ensure repair kit is complete and accessible

### During the Race
- **Start conservatively**: Don't get caught up in the excitement and burn out
- **Watch for hazards**: Roots, rocks, mud, water crossings, other teams
- **Communicate constantly**: Warn teammates about obstacles, turns, stops
- **Handle mechanicals calmly**: Don't panic—fix issues methodically

### Bike Security at Checkpoints
- If you need to leave bikes to hike to a checkpoint:
  - Lock or hide bikes if race rules allow
  - Remember exactly where you left them
  - Take navigation tools with you

<br/>

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Riding too hard early**: Save energy for the full race
2. **Inadequate repair kit**: Missing key tools or spares
3. **Poor bike fit**: Causes discomfort and inefficiency over long distances
4. **Not practicing mechanical repairs**: Knowing how to fix problems saves hours
5. **Ignoring nutrition**: Bonking on the bike is preventable
6. **Separating from teammates**: Stay together—penalties aren't worth it
7. **Wrong gear for terrain**: Gear up before hills, not during them
8. **Skipping training on the bike**: Road riding doesn't prepare you for technical trails

<br/>

## Summary

Mountain biking in adventure racing is about efficiency, reliability, and teamwork—not speed. By choosing the right bike, setting it up properly, carrying essential repair tools, pacing yourself wisely, and working well with your team, you'll maximize your performance and enjoyment on the bike legs.

Remember: the fastest bike leg is the one where you don't have a mechanical, don't get separated from your team, and still have energy for the next discipline. Ride smart, stay together, and keep moving forward.
