# Getting Started with Adventure Racing

Adventure racing combines navigation, endurance, teamwork, and outdoor problem-solving. This page is a quick-start guide to getting to your first finish line (without overthinking it).

## Is adventure racing right for you?

Adventure racing is a good fit if you like:

- Moving outdoors (trek, bike, paddle)
- Solving problems while tired
- Working as a team

You don’t need to be elite or have perfect skills on day one. Start small, learn fast.

## Step 1: Pick the right first race

For most beginners, a **sprint (2–4 hours)** is ideal.

Look for:

- Beginner or open categories
- Clear rules and mandatory gear list
- A location you can reach without major travel stress

> [!TIP]
> Volunteering at a local race is an underrated first step: you’ll learn transitions, see typical gear, and meet teams.

## Step 2: Build a team you’ll still like at hour 3

Most races are 2–4 people. For your first race, prioritize:

- Positive attitude and willingness to learn
- Similar “finish and learn” goals
- Reliability (shows up, trains a little, communicates)

Before race day, agree on:

- Goal (finish vs. compete)
- Who is primary navigator vs. checker
- Budget and gear plan

For deeper teamwork guidance, see [Team Dynamics](/content/team-dynamics.md).

## Step 3: Learn just enough skills to have fun

Minimum viable skills for a first sprint:

- **Basic navigation workflow** (plan → execute → confirm → adjust)
- **Pacing** (steady beats heroic early speed)
- **Simple problem solving** (quick resets when things go sideways)

Keep navigation conceptual here and go deeper later: [Navigation](/content/navigation.md).

## Step 4: Train (lightly) and don’t get injured

If you can comfortably do a few hours of outdoor movement, you can finish a sprint.

Simple approach for 6–10 weeks:

- 1 longer easy session each week (trek or bike)
- 1–2 shorter sessions (anything)
- 1 strength/mobility session (optional but helpful)
- At least 1 rest day

If you want discipline-specific ideas, start here:

- [Trekking](/content/trekking.md)
- [Biking](/content/biking.md)
- [Paddling](/content/paddling.md)

## Step 5: Gear (keep it simple)

Your race director’s mandatory gear list wins over any internet list.

For most beginner sprints, plan for:

- Clothing for being wet/cold longer than expected
- A way to carry water + food
- Basic safety items (light, whistle, small first aid)

Detailed checklists live in [Gear](/content/gear.md) and [Nutrition & Hydration](/content/nutrition.md).

## Step 6: Race week and race day

Race week:

- Sleep, eat normally, and reduce training volume
- Read the race packet and mandatory gear list
- Make a simple plan for logistics and roles

Race day fundamentals:

- Start steady
- Stay in map contact and confirm often
- Eat and drink early (don’t “save” food for later)
- Communicate uncertainty early
- Stay together

## Step 7: After the race (the real improvement happens here)

Within 48 hours:

- Recover (food, hydration, sleep)
- Write down what worked and what didn’t
- Pick one improvement for the next race (navigation, pacing, gear, or teamwork)

## Common first-race questions

**How fit do I need to be?**
If you can do a few hours outside at an easy pace, you can finish a sprint.

**What if we get lost?**
It happens. Stop early, reset to a known feature, and solve it as a team.

**How much does it cost?**
Varies by region and race. Entry fees are often the main cost; borrowing gear is common for your first event.

**Do we need to be good at every discipline?**
No. Beginner races are designed for learning.
