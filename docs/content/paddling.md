# Paddling in Adventure Racing

## Introduction

Paddling is often the most intimidating discipline for new adventure racers, yet it can also be one of the most rewarding. Whether in canoes, kayaks, or packrafts, paddling sections offer a unique challenge that combines physical endurance, technical skill, navigation, and teamwork—all while on the water.

Unlike recreational paddling, adventure racing paddling involves navigating to checkpoints, managing time pressure, coordinating with teammates in close quarters, and sometimes portaging (carrying) your boat between water sections. This guide covers everything beginners need to know to tackle paddling legs with confidence.

## Types of Boats Used in Adventure Racing

### Canoes
**Design**: Open-top boats with high sides
**Typical Capacity**: 2-3 people plus gear
**Paddling Style**: Usually single-bladed paddles (one per person), though some racers use double-bladed kayak paddles

**Pros**:
- Excellent for team coordination
- Spacious for gear storage
- Stable on flatwater
- Good for rivers and lakes

**Cons**:
- Less efficient in wind
- Can be heavy to portage
- Slower in choppy water
- Requires good tandem coordination

**Best For**: Sprint to 12-hour races with established water sections, team paddling

### Kayaks
**Design**: Enclosed or sit-on-top boats with lower profile
**Typical Capacity**: 1-2 people (tandem kayaks for team races)
**Paddling Style**: Double-bladed paddles

**Pros**:
- More efficient than canoes in wind and waves
- Faster on open water
- Better in choppy conditions
- Good tracking (stays straight)

**Cons**:
- Less gear storage than canoes
- Heavier to carry than packrafts
- Can be harder to enter/exit
- Tandem coordination takes practice

**Best For**: Races with longer paddling sections, windy or choppy water conditions

### Packrafts
**Design**: Ultra-lightweight inflatable rafts
**Weight**: Typically 5-8 lbs (vs. 40-80 lbs for traditional boats)
**Capacity**: Usually 1 person

**Pros**:
- Pack into a backpack when deflated
- Extremely light for portaging
- Perfect for mixed terrain races (bike-hike-paddle transitions)
- Surprisingly durable despite light weight
- Easy to transport to races

**Cons**:
- Slower than canoes/kayaks
- More affected by wind
- Require inflation (5-10 minutes)
- Can be tippy until you get used to them
- More expensive than rental options

**Best For**: Long expedition races, races with multiple short water sections, remote races requiring gear portability

### Race-Provided Boats
Many races provide canoes or kayaks at paddling transition areas. This eliminates the need to own and transport boats, making adventure racing more accessible to beginners.

**Pros**: No ownership or transport required
**Cons**: May be heavier or less well-maintained; you don't get to practice with the exact boat

## Basic Paddling Technique

### Forward Stroke (Most Important)
1. **Sit upright** with good posture
2. **Rotate your torso** as you reach forward (don't just use arms)
3. **Plant the paddle fully** in the water near your feet
4. **Pull toward your hip** using torso rotation, not just arm strength
5. **Exit cleanly** when paddle reaches your hip, don't drag past
6. **Repeat** on opposite side

**Key Point**: Power comes from torso rotation and core, not just arms. Using proper technique prevents fatigue and blisters.

### Sweep Stroke (For Turning)
- **Wide, arcing stroke** from front to back of boat
- Used to turn the boat left or right
- Stern (back) paddler uses this most for steering

### Reverse Stroke
- Same as forward stroke, but paddling backward
- Used to slow down or back up
- Essential for maneuvering in tight spaces

### Draw Stroke
- Pull paddle sideways toward the boat to move boat sideways
- Useful for docking or avoiding obstacles

### Rudder Stroke (Canoes)
- Paddle held in water at an angle behind boat
- Used by stern paddler to steer while maintaining forward momentum

### Reading Water
- **Current**: Water flows faster on outside of river bends
- **Eddies**: Calm pockets behind rocks or points—useful for resting
- **Obstacles**: Watch for rocks, logs, and shallow areas
- **Wind**: Can push you off course—anticipate and correct constantly

## Safety Essentials

### Mandatory Safety Gear
**Personal Flotation Device (PFD)**:
- Must be worn at all times on the water (race requirement)
- Should fit snugly and not ride up when in water
- Choose paddling-specific PFDs for better mobility

**Helmet**:
- Required for whitewater or any sections with rapids
- Protects against rocks, boat impacts, and capsizes

**Whistle**:
- Attached to PFD for emergencies
- Three short blasts = distress signal

### Additional Safety Items
- **Throw bag** (rescue rope): For helping other paddlers or self-rescue
- **Spare paddle**: Can be shared among team or stored in boat
- **Knife or line cutter**: To cut tangled lines in emergency
- **First aid kit**: Waterproofed and accessible
- **Repair kit**: Especially critical for packrafts (patch kit, inflation bag)

### Clothing for Paddling
**Dress for Immersion**: Assume you'll get wet and cold

**Warm Weather**:
- Synthetic or quick-dry materials
- Avoid cotton (stays wet and cold)
- Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, long sleeves

**Cold Weather**:
- Wetsuit or drysuit for cold water (below 60°F)
- Layered synthetic or wool clothing
- Waterproof jacket
- Gloves (paddling gloves or neoprene)
- Spare dry clothes in waterproof bag

**Footwear**:
- Water shoes or sandals with straps (not flip-flops)
- Neoprene booties for cold water

### Capsize and Self-Rescue
**If You Capsize**:
1. **Stay calm** and hold onto paddle
2. **Stay with the boat** unless in danger
3. **Get to shore or shallow water** as quickly as safe
4. **Empty boat** and re-enter if conditions allow, or swim it to shore
5. **Check on teammates** and help as needed

**Prevention**:
- Keep weight low and centered in boat
- Move slowly when shifting positions
- Lean into turns, not away
- Be extra careful in wind and waves

## Team Coordination in Boats

### Roles in Tandem Boats

**Bow Paddler (Front)**:
- Provides power and rhythm
- Often handles navigation (map reading)
- Calls out obstacles ahead
- Sets the stroke rate

**Stern Paddler (Back)**:
- Steers the boat
- Matches bow paddler's rhythm
- Makes final directional decisions
- More experienced paddler typically sits here

### Communication
- **Before launch**: Agree on roles, navigation strategy, and hand signals
- **During paddling**: Call out turns, obstacles, and checkpoints
- **Stroke rhythm**: "Switch" or "Hut" to change paddle sides together (optional)
- **Rest breaks**: Agree when to stop paddling vs. keep moving

### Synchronization
- **Match cadence**: Paddle at the same rate to maximize efficiency
- **Switch sides together**: Prevents boat from veering
- **Share the work**: Take turns navigating or steering to prevent fatigue

### Navigation on Water
- **Waterproof map case**: Essential for keeping map dry and readable
- **Compass**: More reliable than GPS on water
- **Landmarks**: Identify points on shore to track progress
- **Checkpoint strategy**: Plan route before launching; stopping mid-paddle wastes time

Learn more about navigation techniques in our [Navigation Guide](/content/navigation.md).

## Portaging (Carrying Your Boat)

### What is Portaging?
Carrying your boat and gear over land between water sections or around obstacles (rapids, dams, fallen trees).

### Efficient Portaging Techniques

**For Canoes and Kayaks**:
- **Two-person carry**: One at bow, one at stern; lift together on count
- **Overhead carry** (canoes): Flip boat upside down, rest on shoulders using yoke or pads
- **Practice**: Learn lifting and carrying techniques before race day to avoid injury

**For Packrafts**:
- **Deflate and pack**: Roll tightly and secure with straps
- **Pack in backpack**: Many packrafts fit in or attach to small packs
- **Huge advantage**: Much faster than carrying heavy boats

### Organizing Gear for Portages
- **Dry bags**: Keep all gear organized and waterproof
- **Minimize items**: Don't bring unnecessary gear into the boat
- **Quick access**: Keep frequently-needed items (map, snacks, repair kit) accessible
- **Load distribution**: Balance weight in boat for better handling

## Training for Paddling

### Build Paddling Fitness
- **Time on water**: Even casual paddling builds essential muscles
- **Practice sessions**: Aim for 1-2 hours at sustained pace
- **Core and upper body strength**: Planks, pull-ups, and rotational exercises
- **Cardio base**: Running and biking support overall endurance

### Develop Skills
- **Take a course**: Even a 2-hour beginner lesson teaches proper technique
- **Practice strokes**: Master forward stroke, sweep, and rudder/J-stroke
- **Learn self-rescue**: Practice capsizing and re-entering in safe conditions
- **Try different boats**: Experience canoes, kayaks, and packrafts if possible

### Team Practice
- **Paddle together**: Build communication and rhythm with your race teammates
- **Practice portaging**: Learn to lift, carry, and pack efficiently
- **Navigate on water**: Combine paddling with map reading and checkpoint finding

### Race Simulation
- **Paddle after biking or trekking**: Simulate the fatigue of multi-sport racing
- **Cold or wet conditions**: Train in challenging weather to build confidence
- **Time-based goals**: Practice maintaining pace for 1-2 hours straight

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

### 1. Underestimating Paddling
**Mistake**: Assuming paddling is easy because it looks relaxing
**Solution**: Practice paddling several times before race day; build technique and endurance

### 2. Poor Stroke Technique
**Mistake**: Using only arms instead of torso rotation; death grip on paddle
**Solution**: Take a lesson or watch instructional videos; practice correct form

### 3. Lack of Navigation Practice
**Mistake**: Struggling to read map in boat or getting disoriented on water
**Solution**: Use waterproof map case; practice identifying landmarks before launching

### 4. Inadequate Safety Gear
**Mistake**: Skipping PFD, helmet, or spare paddle to save weight
**Solution**: Always wear required safety gear—rules exist for good reasons

### 5. Poor Packing and Waterproofing
**Mistake**: Gear gets soaked, causing hypothermia or equipment failure
**Solution**: Use quality dry bags; double-bag electronics and critical items

### 6. Inefficient Portaging
**Mistake**: Struggling with awkward carries or dropping boat
**Solution**: Practice portaging techniques; learn proper lifting form

### 7. Not Communicating
**Mistake**: Bow and stern paddlers not synchronized; missed turns or obstacles
**Solution**: Establish communication plan before launching; talk constantly

### 8. Ignoring Weather Conditions
**Mistake**: Paddling in high wind or dangerous conditions without adjusting strategy
**Solution**: Monitor wind and waves; take breaks in lee of shore; consider alternate routes

### 9. Rushing Transitions
**Mistake**: Launching unprepared or forgetting gear in boat after landing
**Solution**: Take 30 seconds to organize; do gear check before and after paddling

## Paddling Strategy for Races

### Pre-Launch
- **Study the map**: Identify checkpoints, potential hazards, and bailout points
- **Agree on route**: Discuss with team before getting in boat
- **Organize gear**: Everything in dry bags, critical items accessible
- **Check boat**: Ensure no damage; verify paddles and safety gear

### During Paddling
- **Start conservatively**: Find sustainable pace in first 10 minutes
- **Frequent navigation checks**: Confirm position every 5-10 minutes
- **Monitor teammates**: Watch for signs of fatigue or cold
- **Fuel and hydrate**: Easier to eat/drink on water than while trekking
- **Stay together**: If racing solo boats (e.g., packrafts), maintain visual contact

### Post-Paddle
- **Secure boat**: Follow race rules for leaving/storing boat
- **Quick gear change**: Don't linger—move efficiently to next discipline
- **Warm up if cold**: Change into dry layers immediately

## Summary

Paddling in adventure racing is a unique challenge that requires a blend of physical fitness, technical skill, navigation ability, and teamwork. By choosing the right boat, learning proper technique, prioritizing safety, and practicing with your team, you'll transform paddling from an intimidating obstacle into an enjoyable and efficient part of your race.

Remember: The goal isn't to be an expert paddler—it's to move safely and efficiently from checkpoint to checkpoint while staying with your team. Even basic skills and a positive attitude will carry you through successfully.
