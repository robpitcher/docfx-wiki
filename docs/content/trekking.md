# Trekking in Adventure Racing

<br/>

## What is Trekking in Adventure Racing?

Trekking (or foot travel) is often the most demanding discipline in adventure racing. Unlike a traditional trail run, trekking sections combine trail running, power hiking, bushwhacking through unmarked terrain, and off-trail navigation—often while carrying a pack and racing for hours or even days. Success in trekking requires not just fitness, but also smart pacing, solid navigation skills, proper footwear, diligent foot care, and excellent teamwork.

<br/>

## Trail Running vs. Race Trekking

### The Reality of Race Trekking

Don't expect to run the entire trekking section. Adventure racing trekking involves:
- **Mixed movement**: Running, hiking, and sometimes crawling through difficult terrain
- **Power hiking**: Walking efficiently uphill or through technical sections—even elite racers power hike
- **Variable terrain**: Rocks, mud, steep climbs, descents, creek crossings, and dense vegetation
- **Navigation while moving**: Constantly checking your map and making route decisions

> [!TIP]
> Use trekking sections to rehearse your navigation chatter—call out bearings and landmarks even in training.

### Embrace the Hike
There's no shame in hiking, especially on steep inclines or rough terrain. Power hiking—walking with purpose and good form—is often more efficient than struggling to run. Save your running energy for flat or gently rolling terrain where it makes sense.

### Building Your Base
Train for trekking by:
- **Logging time on feet**: Focus on long, slow distance to condition legs and feet
- **Training on varied terrain**: Seek out trails with rocks, roots, hills, and technical features
- **Carrying weight**: Train with a loaded pack (10-15 lbs) to simulate race conditions
- **Practicing navigation**: Combine training hikes with map-reading practice

## Bushwhacking and Off-Trail Navigation

### What is Bushwhacking?
Bushwhacking means traveling through unmarked wilderness—no trails, just you, your team, and the terrain. This might involve:
- Pushing through dense brush or undergrowth
- Crossing boulder fields or scree slopes
- Following faint animal trails or game paths
- Navigating around obstacles like cliffs, thick vegetation, or swamps

### Techniques for Efficient Bushwhacking
- **Look for natural corridors**: Creek beds (if dry), ridgelines, or clearings often provide easier passage
- **Use handrails**: Follow linear features like streams, fences, or ridges to maintain direction
- **Spread out slightly**: Team members can find the best path through brush, then call others to follow
- **Be patient**: Fighting thick brush burns energy fast—sometimes a slight detour around dense areas is faster
- **Protect your face and hands**: Long sleeves help prevent scratches from branches

### Navigation While Trekking
Effective navigation requires:
- **Frequent map checks**: Confirm your location every few minutes, especially in featureless terrain
- **Terrain association**: Match what you see (hills, valleys, water features) to your map
- **Dead reckoning**: Walk on a compass bearing for a known distance when crossing featureless areas
- **Attack points**: Navigate to a large, obvious feature near your checkpoint, then fine-tune from there
- **Relocation techniques**: If lost, identify surrounding features and work backward to find yourself

Learn more about navigation in our [Navigation Guide](/content/navigation.md).

## Footwear and Foot Care

### Choosing the Right Footwear

**Trail Running Shoes**:
- Lightweight and comfortable for running sections
- Good drainage for wet conditions
- Lower ankle support but more agile
- Best for: Sprint races, less technical terrain, experienced runners

**Light Hiking Boots**:
- More ankle support for rough terrain
- Better protection from rocks and roots
- Slightly heavier but more durable
- Best for: Longer races, heavy packs, less experienced trekkers

**Key Features to Look For**:
- **Drainage**: Shoes that shed water quickly (wet feet are inevitable)
- **Grip**: Aggressive tread for mud, rocks, and wet surfaces
- **Fit**: Snug heel, room in toe box (feet swell during long efforts)
- **Durability**: Reinforced toe caps and sidewalls

### Sock Selection
- **Avoid cotton**: Cotton stays wet and causes blisters
- **Use synthetic or merino wool**: These materials wick moisture and dry faster
- **Consider double-layer socks**: They reduce friction and can prevent blisters
- **Bring spares**: Pack 1-2 extra pairs in your gear bins for longer races

### Essential Foot Care

**Before the Race**:
- Train in your race shoes to break them in
- Toughen feet gradually with increasing mileage on varied terrain
- Trim toenails (but not too short) a few days before the race

**During the Race**:
- **Address hot spots immediately**: The moment you feel friction or a hot spot, stop and apply tape (Leukotape or athletic tape)
- **Change socks at transitions**: Dry socks can prevent blisters and boost morale
- **Air out feet**: Remove shoes at TAs when possible to dry feet and check for problems
- **Use lubrication**: Some racers apply Vaseline, Body Glide, or foot powder to reduce friction

**Blister Management**:
- Carry blister supplies: tape, moleskin, small scissors
- Drain blisters if large (sterilize needle, drain fluid, cover with tape)
- Never ignore foot pain—small problems become race-ending issues

## Pacing Strategy

### Think "All-Day Pace"
Adventure racing is not about speed—it's about sustainable, continuous forward movement. Your goal is to maintain steady effort for hours.

**Principles of Good Pacing**:
- **Start conservatively**: It's easy to go too hard in the first hour when fresh
- **Find your rhythm**: Settle into a pace you can maintain indefinitely
- **Adjust for terrain**: Slow down on climbs and technical sections; move efficiently on flat ground
- **Monitor your team**: Pace to the slowest member—burning them out early hurts everyone

### Using Trekking Poles
Trekking poles can be highly beneficial:
- Reduce impact on knees during descents
- Provide extra push on climbs
- Improve balance on technical terrain or creek crossings
- Distribute effort across upper body

However, poles can be cumbersome when bushwhacking or scrambling. Decide based on terrain and personal preference.

## Nutrition and Hydration While Trekking

### Fuel Continuously
Don't wait until you're hungry or tired:
- **Eat small amounts frequently**: Every 20-30 minutes
- **Mix food types**: Combine quick carbs (gels, chews) with longer-lasting foods (bars, nuts, sandwiches)
- **Practice during training**: Learn what your stomach tolerates during sustained effort

### Stay Hydrated
- Drink regularly, not just when thirsty
- Carry at least 1-2 liters of water on trekking legs
- Plan water sources (streams, lakes) on your route and carry purification tablets or a filter
- Monitor urine color—clear to light yellow is ideal

Learn more about race nutrition in our [Nutrition Guide](/content/nutrition.md).

## Night Trekking

### Added Challenges
Trekking at night is significantly harder:
- **Navigation is more difficult**: Terrain features are harder to identify
- **Pacing shifts**: Easier to move too slowly or rush carelessly
- **Fatigue compounds**: Mental and physical exhaustion peak during night hours
- **Cold temperatures**: Nights can be much colder, requiring extra layers

### Essential Night Trekking Tips

**Lighting**:
- Use a high-quality headlamp (300+ lumens) with fresh batteries
- Always carry a backup headlamp or extra batteries
- Adjust brightness based on terrain—save battery when possible

**Navigation**:
- Check your map more frequently
- Confirm your position using obvious terrain features
- Take bearings carefully and double-check them
- Move more slowly to avoid navigation errors

**Team Movement**:
- **Stay close together**: Easier to lose teammates in darkness
- **Use a sweeper**: Assign the last person to watch for anyone falling behind
- **Communicate frequently**: Call out obstacles, stops, or route changes
- **Designate rest breaks**: Plan short stops to regroup, check maps, and boost morale

**Safety**:
- Watch for tripping hazards (roots, rocks, holes)
- Use trekking poles for stability
- Take care on steep descents—falls are more likely in darkness

## Moving Efficiently as a Team

### Core Principles

**Stay Together**:
- Most races require teams to remain within 100 meters of each other
- Staying close improves safety, navigation, and morale
- Stragglers slow the entire team—adjust pace to keep everyone together

**Share the Load**:
- Rotate navigation duties to prevent mental fatigue
- Stronger members carry extra weight from struggling teammates
- Distribute tasks: one navigates, another manages food, someone tracks time

**Use Tow Systems**:
- A simple bungee cord or rope can help a lagging teammate without verbal pressure
- The stronger member clips in front, providing gentle assistance
- Preserves team dynamics by avoiding constant "hurry up" comments

**Minimize Stops**:
- Keep moving during navigation checks when possible
- Eat and drink on the go
- Save longer stops for transition areas

**Communicate Constantly**:
- Share navigation observations: "I see that hill on the left"
- Call out obstacles: "Root ahead" or "Creek crossing"
- Check in on teammates: "Everyone good?" or "Need to stop?"
- Make group decisions: "Should we follow the ridge or drop to the creek?"

### Building Team Trekking Skills
- **Practice together**: Train as a team to learn each other's strengths and weaknesses
- **Rotate roles**: Everyone should learn to navigate, not just one person
- **Develop communication shortcuts**: Use simple, clear signals and phrases
- **Support each other mentally**: Encourage struggling teammates and stay positive

Learn more about teamwork in our [Team Dynamics Guide](/content/team-dynamics.md).

## Training for Trekking

### Build Endurance
- **Long, slow distance**: Regular hikes/runs of 2-4+ hours at conversational pace
- **Back-to-back days**: Train on consecutive days to simulate multi-leg racing
- **Loaded carries**: Hike with a weighted pack (10-20 lbs) to condition shoulders and legs

### Develop Strength
- **Hill training**: Run or hike repeats on steep inclines
- **Core work**: Planks, side planks, and rotational exercises for pack carrying
- **Leg strength**: Squats, lunges, and step-ups for climbing and descents

### Practice Skills
- **Off-trail movement**: Seek out rough terrain, boulder fields, and thick brush
- **Night training**: Practice trekking with headlamps in safe, familiar areas
- **Navigation**: Combine training sessions with map and compass practice

### Simulate Race Conditions
- Train in varied weather (rain, cold, heat)
- Practice eating and drinking while moving
- Trek with your team to build chemistry and communication

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Starting too fast**: Save energy for later hours when fatigue sets in
2. **Ignoring foot problems**: Small hot spots become major blisters
3. **Poor nutrition**: Bonking (running out of energy) is preventable with consistent fueling
4. **Over-relying on one navigator**: Spread the mental load across the team
5. **Fighting the terrain**: Sometimes a detour is faster than forcing through obstacles
6. **Not training specificity**: Road running doesn't prepare you for rocky, muddy trails
7. **Neglecting night practice**: Night trekking is dramatically different from daytime

<br/>

## Summary

Trekking is the heart of adventure racing—it's where navigation, endurance, and teamwork all come together. By choosing the right footwear, caring for your feet, pacing smartly, and moving efficiently as a team, you'll maximize your performance and enjoyment. Remember: it's not about running every step, it's about continuous forward progress, staying together, and reaching each checkpoint safely.
